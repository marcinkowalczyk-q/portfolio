## Context

e-Delivery is a public digital service that enables legally binding electronic communication between citizens, companies, and public institutions.

The service was designed to work as a digital equivalent of a registered letter with confirmation of receipt, while also using patterns familiar to users from email inboxes and the mObywatel ecosystem.

## Challenge

The biggest challenge was translating the complex logic of mailboxes, roles, and access permissions into an interface that users could understand without knowing the legal and system rules behind the service.

The product had to help users understand which context they were acting in, which mailbox they had access to, what permissions they had, and what consequences their actions could have.

## Working within constraints

The project was developed as part of a public digital service, with constraints resulting from legal requirements, accessibility standards, integration with ePUAP, and the mObywatel ecosystem.

During the project, the design direction also changed: the initial, more custom UI solutions had to be adapted to the style and patterns of mObywatel, while still preserving the complex logic of areas such as permissions, roles, and mailbox settings.

---

## Understanding the context of a public service

The key problem was not the message inbox itself, but the control layer around it: selecting the right mailbox, recognizing the user’s role, accessing specific features, and understanding the consequences of performed actions.

e-Delivery had to feel familiar, but it operated according to the rules of formal communication with public institutions, where message status, access, and permissions have real meaning.

---

## From a simple inbox to a complex access model

At the interface level, e-Delivery could look like a regular message inbox. But behind simple user actions, there was much more complex logic: multiple mailbox types, different roles, delegated access, folders, settings, and the coexistence of e-Delivery and ePUAP.

My work was not about defining this logic from scratch, but about understanding it together with analysts and translating it into the structure of the interface: users, mailboxes, roles, resources, and available actions. As a result, individual screens could follow one consistent service model instead of working as separate, disconnected views.

The dashboard served as an entry point to different communication contexts: personal, business, and shared mailboxes, as well as areas connected with e-Delivery and ePUAP. The goal was to make it clear which mailbox the user was using, on whose behalf they were acting, and what actions were available.

## Permissions model as system logic

In the permissions area, the most important part was understanding the relationship between the user, role, folder, and available actions. This logic was largely defined by the system, so the design work was about translating it into the interface structure rather than creating the rules from scratch.

In practice, this meant breaking down a complex access model into elements that users could understand more easily: who has access, in which role, to which parts of the mailbox, and what they can do there. As a result, the permissions area could work as a control tool, not just a list of technical settings.

## Mailbox settings as a security layer

In e-Delivery, notifications were not just a minor user preference. They helped make sure users would not miss important correspondence assigned to a specific mailbox.

![Mailbox settings, Notifications tab: a form for the email address that receives e-Delivery notifications](/images/e-doreczenia/notification-settings.png)

## System interface layers

e-Delivery isn't a single screen but several connected areas. On the dashboard, users choose a mailbox; in the mailbox, they work with messages; in permissions, they decide who can access what; and in settings, they control how the mailbox works.

These areas depend on each other. What a user can do depends on which mailbox they're in, what role they have and what permissions they hold. That's why I designed them as one system rather than a set of separate screens.

## Accessibility and collaboration

As a public service, e-Delivery had to meet WCAG. I was responsible for accessibility at the design level: contrast, the type scale and the legibility of interface states. Focus states were already provided by the design system. A WCAG specialist, working with the developers, verified the implementation: keyboard support, screen reader behaviour and ARIA attributes. I based the design on usability research run by the research team and on input from the business-technical analyst, within technical constraints and legal requirements.

---

## Key takeaways

Working on e-Delivery showed me that in complex public services, clarity often does not come from individual screens, but from the logic those screens need to represent.

The biggest design value was translating predefined system rules, roles, and access dependencies into an interface that allowed users to act safely and intuitively within the right mailbox context.

- Familiar patterns lower the entry barrier, but they are not enough to explain the formal logic of the service.
- The roles and permissions model needs to be designed as a system of dependencies, not as a single screen.
- Collaboration with analysts was crucial, because UI decisions were driven by legal and system logic.
